The Foundation

Good negotiation is collaborative rather than a contract designed to pressure someone into agreeing. The goal is clarity, not persuasion.

Consent

Freely given

Everyone should be able to say yes, no, not now, or change their mind without punishment, pressure, or retaliation.

Specificity

Make it concrete

“I like bondage” leaves many questions unanswered. Discuss what, where, how intense, for how long, and under what conditions.

Reversibility

Easy to stop

Build clear stop signals and practical ways to pause or end the activity. A person should never have to negotiate their way out of a scene.

Honesty

Disclose relevant information

Share information that could materially affect safety or consent, including injuries, medications, allergies, experience level, and relevant emotional triggers.

Capacity

Know when not to proceed

Intoxication, severe distress, impaired judgment, or inability to communicate reliably can make meaningful consent impossible.

Review

Re-negotiate

Preferences evolve. A negotiation should be revisited when partners, activities, circumstances, or risk factors change.

What to negotiate before a scene

What are we doing?

Define the activities in plain language. Avoid assuming that a label means the same thing to both people.

What is the desired experience?

Discuss the goal: intimacy, challenge, sensation, roleplay, ritual, surrender, learning, or something else.

What is off-limits?

Identify hard limits and activities that require conditions, lower intensity, or explicit check-ins.

How will we communicate?

Choose safewords, nonverbal signals, check-in methods, and what different signals mean.

What are the practical constraints?

Discuss time, location, privacy, equipment, clothing, cleanup, interruptions, transportation, and who else may be present.

What happens afterward?

Talk about aftercare, decompression, hydration, food, sleep, emotional support, and when you will check in again.

Limits, preferences, and conditions

Not every “no” has the same meaning, and not every “yes” is unconditional.

CategoryMeaningUseful question
Hard limitAn activity the person does not consent to.“What should never happen?”
Soft limitAn activity that may be acceptable only with specific boundaries or conditions.“What would make this acceptable or unacceptable?”
PreferenceSomething enjoyable but not essential.“What would you like more of?”
Conditional yesConsent depends on a stated condition.“What must be true before this is okay?”
CuriosityInteresting to discuss, but not necessarily consented to try.“Is this a discussion-only topic or something you want to explore?”
Important distinction: “Maybe,” “I don't know,” or “I'm nervous” is not a green light. Treat uncertainty as a reason to slow down and talk, not as permission to proceed.

Risk-aware negotiation

Discuss risk in proportion to the activity. The more consequential the potential harm, the more specific the planning should be.

Talk about

  • Relevant medical or physical limitations
  • Past injuries and areas that should be protected
  • Allergies, skin sensitivities, or equipment concerns
  • Emotional or psychological triggers that matter to the scene
  • Emergency contacts and access to help when appropriate
  • What signs mean “slow down,” “check in,” or “stop”

Plan for failure

  • What if a safeword cannot be spoken?
  • What if communication becomes unclear?
  • What if equipment fails or becomes uncomfortable?
  • What if someone becomes unexpectedly distressed?
  • Who can end the scene and how?
  • What is the default response when something feels wrong?
Red flag: If a proposed activity carries a serious possibility of lasting injury, loss of consciousness, or inability to communicate, do not treat a simple “yes” as sufficient risk planning. Get appropriate, activity-specific education and establish a conservative stop plan.

Safewords & stop signals

Traffic-light system

Green = continue / good.

Yellow = pause, reduce intensity, or check in.

Red = stop the activity.

Blue = medical/phychological issue.

Nonverbal signals

Agree on a reliable signal if speech may become difficult. Examples include dropping an object, tapping, or another unmistakable signal.

Silence is not consent

Decide in advance what happens if someone stops responding normally. A negotiated scene should have a conservative default: pause and assess.

Make signals unambiguous. Avoid a system where the person receiving the signal has to guess whether it means “stop.” If there is doubt, pause.

Aftercare is part of negotiation

Aftercare is not one universal routine. Ask what helps each person return to baseline and agree on what support is wanted.

Physical

Water, food (chocolate works great for many), warmth, comfortable clothing, rest, cuddles, or practical care as appropriate.

Emotional

Quiet time, affection, reassurance, conversation, space, or another agreed form of decompression.

Follow-up

Decide whether you want a later check-in. Some reactions appear after the scene rather than immediately. Consider multiple days of check-ins.

Common questions

Do I need a formal contract?

No. A written agreement can be useful for remembering details, but it does not create permanent consent or prevent either person from changing their mind.

Can negotiation happen immediately before a scene?

Yes, for straightforward activities. More complex or higher-risk activities deserve enough time for thoughtful discussion, questions, and preparation rather than rushed consent.

What if my partner changes their mind?

Stop or modify the activity as requested. A change of mind is a normal part of consent.

Should I negotiate intensity?

Yes. “Impact,” “restraint,” “roleplay,” or another activity can cover a wide range of experiences. Discuss desired intensity, pace, duration, and boundaries rather than relying on labels.

What if we discover a new boundary during the scene?

Stop or pause and reassess. New information can change the negotiation. There is no requirement to finish what was originally planned.
