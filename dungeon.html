Dungeon Etiquette: How to Be a Good Guest in a Play Space

Whether you are visiting a dungeon for the first time, attending a play party, participating in a workshop, or returning to a familiar community, understanding dungeon etiquette is an important part of being a responsible member of the BDSM community.

A dungeon is more than a room filled with equipment. It is a shared environment where people explore vulnerability, trust, power exchange, sensation, and connection. Some people may be participating in intense scenes, while others are learning, observing, socializing, or simply enjoying the atmosphere.

The foundation of good dungeon etiquette is simple: respect other people's boundaries, respect their scenes, respect the space, and recognize that consent applies to everything you do.

Every dungeon and event may have its own house rules. Always familiarize yourself with those rules and follow the guidance of the organizers and dungeon monitors. This guide provides general community practices, not a replacement for venue policies.

1. Respect Active Scenes

One of the most important rules of dungeon etiquette is recognizing that an active scene is not a public performance or an invitation to participate.

People engaged in a scene may be experiencing intense physical sensations, emotional vulnerability, altered states of attention, or deep concentration. Interruptions can disrupt the experience and, in some circumstances, create safety concerns.

Things you should avoid

  • Do not interrupt a scene to socialize. Save conversations, introductions, and questions for afterward.
  • Do not offer unsolicited advice. Even if you have experience with a particular activity, an active scene is not the time to critique someone's technique.
  • Do not ask questions during a scene. Questions about equipment, techniques, or what someone is doing can wait.
  • Do not touch participants or their equipment. Never assume that proximity gives you permission to interact with someone or anything involved in their scene.
  • Do not crowd the scene. Give participants room to move and maintain their privacy.
  • Do not make unsolicited commentary. Comments about someone's body, performance, sounds, reactions, or relationship are generally inappropriate.

When is it appropriate to interrupt?

There is an important distinction between interrupting a scene because you are curious and intervening because you believe someone may be in danger.

If you observe an immediate safety concern, a possible consent violation, an apparent medical emergency, or someone who seems unable to communicate or respond, do not simply assume everything is fine.

Get the attention of a dungeon monitor or event organizer. If there is an immediate threat to someone's safety, take appropriate action and seek assistance. Do not let concerns about interrupting a scene prevent you from addressing a genuine emergency.

When in doubt, quietly seek assistance from the people responsible for maintaining the space.

2. Ask Before Touching Anything or Anyone

Consent does not stop at the boundaries of a scene. It applies to every person, piece of equipment, and interaction in a dungeon.

Just because someone is wearing revealing clothing, using BDSM equipment, or participating in a public scene does not mean they have consented to being touched.

Personal boundaries

  • Ask before touching someone, even if you know them.
  • Never touch someone's rope, restraints, collar, or other gear without permission.
  • Do not approach someone from behind and grab or surprise them.
  • Ask before hugging, embracing, or engaging in physical contact.
  • Respect a no without demanding an explanation.
  • Do not assume that a person's submissive or dominant role changes their right to personal boundaries.

Equipment and furniture

Dungeon equipment can be expensive, specialized, and subject to specific safety requirements. A suspension point, St. Andrew's cross, spanking bench, or other piece of furniture is not necessarily available for general use.

  • Ask before using equipment.
  • Ask whether a piece of equipment is reserved for a particular scene.
  • Do not move or adjust someone else's equipment.
  • Never assume you can use another person's personal gear.
  • Follow any posted weight limits, inspection requirements, and venue instructions.
  • Report damaged or potentially unsafe equipment to the organizers.

A simple question such as "Is this available for use?" can prevent an uncomfortable or potentially dangerous situation.

3. Pick-Up Play: Approaching Someone for a Scene

Pick-up play refers to arranging a scene with someone you may not have an established play relationship with. It can be spontaneous, exciting, and a way to meet new people. However, spontaneous does not mean unnegotiated.

A dungeon party is not a place where you can simply walk up to someone and begin touching, restraining, or playing with them.

How to approach someone

  1. Read the situation. Is the person socializing, watching scenes, or otherwise appearing open to conversation? Are they already engaged with someone?
  2. Introduce yourself. A simple introduction and friendly conversation are usually a good starting point.
  3. Ask whether they are interested. Express your interest without assuming it is mutual.
  4. Accept their answer. A no, a maybe, or a lack of interest should not lead to pressure, repeated requests, or negotiation tactics.
  5. Negotiate before playing. If there is mutual interest, take the time to discuss the scene in detail before anything begins.

What should be negotiated?

  • What activities are being considered?
  • What activities are explicitly off-limits?
  • What intensity or level of play is desired?
  • Are there physical limitations, injuries, or other relevant considerations?
  • What safewords or other communication signals will be used?
  • What should happen if someone needs to pause or stop?
  • Are there emotional or psychological considerations?
  • What kind of aftercare might be wanted or needed?
  • Are there privacy expectations, including photographs or recordings?
  • What happens if one person wants to end the scene?

Do not treat a person's willingness to participate in one activity as permission for another. Consent to impact play, for example, is not automatically consent to restraint, humiliation, or any other activity.

Pick-up play is not an excuse to skip experience or safety

Be honest about your experience and abilities. Do not claim expertise you do not have, and do not agree to activities you cannot perform safely.

If you are unfamiliar with a particular activity, say so. Someone who is interested in playing with you should have the information necessary to make an informed decision.

4. When and Where to Negotiate

Negotiation is the conversation that establishes what participants are agreeing to do, what their boundaries are, and how they will communicate throughout a scene.

Negotiation should happen before play begins, not while someone is already restrained, aroused, overwhelmed, or otherwise in a vulnerable position.

Choose an appropriate time and place

  • Find a relatively quiet place where you can communicate without being overheard by everyone around you.
  • Allow enough time to discuss the activity without rushing.
  • Avoid negotiating while someone is actively playing.
  • Do not pressure someone into making a decision because the event is ending or equipment is available.
  • Make sure everyone is able to understand and communicate their decisions.

Negotiation is an ongoing process

A negotiation is not a permanent contract. People can change their minds, discover that something feels different than expected, or decide they no longer want to participate.

Consent can be withdrawn at any point. A person does not need to justify stopping a scene, and stopping should never result in punishment, intimidation, or retaliation.

A safeword or signal is an important communication tool, but it does not replace attentive communication and responsibility from everyone involved.

If alcohol, recreational substances, fatigue, or other factors interfere with someone's ability to understand or communicate consent, do not proceed with a scene.

5. Watching Scenes: Being a Respectful Observer

Watching scenes can be a valuable way to learn about BDSM, see different styles of play, and become more comfortable in a dungeon environment.

However, watching is a privilege, not an entitlement. Participants should be able to enjoy their scenes without feeling like they are being scrutinized or turned into entertainment.

Good observer etiquette

  • Maintain a respectful distance from active scenes.
  • Do not block other people's view or interfere with equipment.
  • Keep conversations and noise away from active scenes.
  • Do not stare in a way that makes participants uncomfortable.
  • Never photograph or record a scene without explicit permission from everyone involved and compliance with venue rules.
  • Do not share details about scenes or participants outside the event without permission.
  • Do not approach participants immediately after a scene to demand an explanation or critique their activities.

Remember that public play does not mean public access

Someone may choose to participate in a scene in a public dungeon environment while still expecting privacy and discretion.

Being allowed to witness a scene does not give you permission to photograph it, record it, share it, or discuss identifying details about the participants.

6. Recognizing Red Flags

A dungeon should be a place where people can explore consensually and feel comfortable establishing boundaries. Unfortunately, not everyone respects those principles.

It is important to recognize behaviors that may indicate someone is unsafe, manipulative, or unwilling to respect consent.

Red flags during negotiation

  • Pressuring someone to agree. Repeatedly asking after someone has declined or attempting to wear down their boundaries.
  • Ignoring stated limits. Treating hard limits as suggestions or trying to persuade someone to change them.
  • Refusing to negotiate. Claiming that experienced players do not need negotiation or that trust alone is sufficient.
  • Using status or experience as leverage. Claiming that a title, reputation, or years in the community entitle someone to special treatment.
  • Discouraging safewords. Suggesting that stopping a scene is weakness or that a submissive should never use a safeword.
  • Misrepresenting experience. Claiming qualifications, knowledge, or abilities that someone does not actually possess.

Red flags during a scene

  • Continuing after someone uses a safeword or stop signal.
  • Ignoring signs of distress or inability to communicate.
  • Introducing activities that were never negotiated.
  • Removing or ignoring agreed safety measures.
  • Using intoxication or vulnerability to obtain agreement.
  • Restricting someone's ability to communicate when that was not agreed upon.
  • Threatening, intimidating, or humiliating someone for establishing boundaries.

Red flags in the community

  • A venue or organizer dismisses reports of consent violations without investigating or addressing concerns.
  • People are pressured to remain silent to protect someone's reputation.
  • A person repeatedly engages in boundary violations but is excused because they are popular or influential.
  • Newcomers are discouraged from asking questions or reporting uncomfortable experiences.
  • Retaliation occurs against people who raise legitimate safety concerns.

A single uncomfortable interaction does not necessarily establish a pattern of misconduct. However, you do not need to prove that someone is dangerous before deciding not to interact or play with them.

Trust your boundaries. If something feels wrong, you are allowed to step away, decline an interaction, and seek support.

7. Reporting Consent Violations and Safety Concerns

Consent violations should be taken seriously. Whether you experienced something yourself, witnessed an incident, or were told about a concerning interaction, there should be a way to bring the matter to the attention of the people responsible for the event.

Reporting is not about creating drama or punishing someone for an awkward interaction. It is about addressing behavior that may put people at risk and helping maintain a safer community.

If you experience a violation

  1. Get somewhere you feel safe. You do not have to continue interacting with the person involved.
  2. Contact a dungeon monitor or organizer. Identify someone responsible for handling safety and consent concerns.
  3. Explain what happened. Share as much detail as you are comfortable providing. You do not need to have every detail organized before asking for help.
  4. Ask about available support. You may want someone to accompany you, help you leave, separate you from another person, or explain the reporting process.
  5. Document what you remember. If appropriate, write down the date, approximate time, location, people involved, witnesses, and what occurred.
  6. Consider additional support. Depending on the incident, you may want medical care, a trusted support person, a specialized support organization, or law enforcement.

If you witness a violation

You do not have to personally investigate or determine whether a violation occurred before bringing a concern to the attention of event staff.

  • If someone appears to be in immediate danger, get help immediately.
  • Notify a dungeon monitor or organizer about what you observed.
  • Describe what you actually saw or heard rather than making assumptions.
  • Provide information about potential witnesses if appropriate.
  • Respect the privacy of the people involved.
  • Do not spread rumors or attempt to organize a public confrontation.

What should event organizers do?

Dungeons and event organizers have an important responsibility to establish clear reporting procedures and respond appropriately when concerns are raised.

A responsible venue should provide:

  • A clearly identified dungeon monitor or designated safety contact.
  • A way to report concerns privately.
  • A process for responding to immediate safety issues.
  • Clear policies regarding harassment, consent violations, and retaliation.
  • Appropriate confidentiality and discretion.
  • A process for reviewing reports and determining appropriate action.
  • Clear expectations about consequences for violating venue rules.

Depending on the circumstances, a venue may need to separate people, restrict participation, remove someone from an event, or take other protective measures.

A report should be taken seriously even if the person reporting it is new to the community or the person being reported is well-known.

Not every concern will result in the same outcome, and organizers should avoid promising absolute confidentiality when they may need to share information to address a safety concern.

Important: Event reporting is not a substitute for emergency assistance. If someone is in immediate danger or needs urgent medical attention, contact emergency services.

8. Respect Privacy and Confidentiality

Privacy is especially important in BDSM communities. People may have personal, professional, family, or social reasons for keeping their participation in kink private.

Never assume that someone is comfortable being publicly identified as a member of the BDSM community.

Protect other people's privacy

  • Do not disclose someone's real name, workplace, or personal information without permission.
  • Do not share photographs, videos, or recordings without explicit permission from everyone identifiable in them.
  • Do not tag people in social media posts without asking.
  • Do not discuss another person's scenes, relationships, or activities outside the community without their permission.
  • Do not assume that someone who attends one public event is comfortable being identified at another.
  • Respect venue policies about phones, cameras, and recording devices.

Confidentiality is a community responsibility. Treat the privacy of others with the same care you would want them to show you.

9. Personal Hygiene and Shared Spaces

Dungeons are shared environments. Good hygiene and respect for common spaces help everyone enjoy the experience.

Basic expectations

  • Arrive clean and wear appropriate clothing for the event.
  • Bring a towel if the venue requires or recommends one.
  • Clean equipment and furniture after use according to venue instructions.
  • Do not assume that a piece of equipment is clean just because it looks clean.
  • Dispose of trash, wipes, and other materials properly.
  • Keep walkways, emergency exits, and access to safety equipment clear.
  • Do not leave personal belongings where they create trip hazards.
  • Respect any food, drink, footwear, and equipment restrictions.

Shared equipment requires shared responsibility

Different materials require different cleaning methods. Leather, wood, metal, fabric, and specialty equipment may have different care requirements.

Follow the venue's cleaning procedures rather than improvising with chemicals or cleaning products that may damage equipment or create safety concerns.

10. Aftercare and What Happens After a Scene

Aftercare is often an important part of a BDSM experience. It may involve physical comfort, emotional reassurance, quiet time, conversation, or simply giving someone space.

However, not everyone wants the same kind of aftercare. Some people prefer conversation and physical closeness, while others may want to decompress independently.

Respect the participants

  • Do not interrupt aftercare to ask questions about the scene.
  • Do not assume someone wants a hug or physical contact.
  • Do not crowd participants who are decompressing.
  • Do not make jokes about emotional reactions or vulnerability.
  • Respect requests for privacy and quiet.
  • Do not assume that an intense scene indicates distress or that a calm reaction means everything was fine.

If you participated in the scene, check in with your partner and follow through on the aftercare you discussed during negotiation.

Aftercare is not a substitute for consent, negotiation, or responsible play. It is one part of the larger process of caring for one another.

11. Be a Good Community Member

A healthy dungeon community depends on more than individual scenes. It depends on people treating one another with respect, being accountable for their behavior, and contributing to an environment where newcomers and experienced players can participate comfortably.

You can help build that environment by:

  • Welcoming newcomers without overwhelming them.
  • Sharing knowledge when someone asks for it.
  • Recognizing that experience does not make someone an authority on everything.
  • Respecting different relationship structures, identities, roles, and styles of play.
  • Being honest about your abilities and limitations.
  • Taking responsibility when you make a mistake.
  • Respecting the venue's rules even when you disagree with them.
  • Supporting people who raise legitimate safety concerns.
  • Understanding that consent and safety are everyone's responsibility.

Nobody expects every interaction to be perfect. What matters is being willing to listen, learn, respect boundaries, and take responsibility for your actions.

12. A Quick Dungeon Etiquette Checklist

Before attending an event, keep these basic principles in mind:

  • Know the venue's rules before participating.
  • Never interrupt an active scene unless there is a legitimate safety concern.
  • Ask before touching anyone or anything.
  • Never assume that watching a scene means you are invited to participate.
  • Negotiate before play begins.
  • Respect safewords, boundaries, and the right to withdraw consent.
  • Never pressure someone into playing.
  • Be honest about your experience and abilities.
  • Protect people's privacy and confidentiality.
  • Clean shared equipment after use.
  • Recognize red flags and report concerning behavior.
  • Know how to contact a dungeon monitor or event organizer.
  • Be respectful of other people's scenes, relationships, and identities.
  • Leave the space as safe and welcoming as you found it.

Final Thoughts: Respect Makes Better Play Possible

A dungeon should be a place where people can explore, experiment, learn, and connect while maintaining their autonomy and personal boundaries.

Good dungeon etiquette is not about memorizing hundreds of arbitrary rules. It is about understanding that your actions affect the people around you.

You do not need to be an experienced player to be a good member of a dungeon community. You need to be respectful, communicate clearly, ask questions when you are unsure, and recognize that consent is fundamental to every interaction.

Whether you are attending your first play party or have been part of the community for decades, the same principle applies:

Respect the people, respect the space, respect the boundaries, and help make the community better for everyone.
