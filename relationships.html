Understanding Relationships and Relationship Styles

Relationships come in many forms, and there is no single structure that works for everyone. People build connections based on their needs, values, attraction, identities, experiences, and personal preferences.

In the BDSM and kink communities, relationship structures can be particularly diverse. Some people practice kink exclusively with a long-term monogamous partner, while others participate in polyamorous relationships, swinging, open relationships, or other forms of consensual non-monogamy.

It is also important to understand that romantic relationships, sexual relationships, emotional connections, friendships, and BDSM dynamics do not necessarily overlap. Someone may have a romantic partner, a sexual partner, a platonic life partner, and a D/s partner, with each connection serving a different purpose in their life.

Understanding these distinctions helps people communicate expectations, establish boundaries, and build relationships that work for everyone involved.

This page explores different relationship structures, the terminology used within consensual non-monogamy, and the differences between romantic, sexual, and platonic connections.

Understanding Relationship Structures

A relationship structure describes how people organize their romantic, sexual, emotional, and other intimate relationships. These structures may involve exclusivity, multiple partners, shared commitments, or different levels of independence.

Some structures are relatively straightforward, while others can involve several interconnected relationships and agreements. No structure is automatically healthier or more successful than another.

Monogamy

Monogamy is a relationship structure in which two people agree to maintain an exclusive romantic and/or sexual relationship with one another.

Traditional monogamy generally involves romantic and sexual exclusivity. However, individual couples may define their agreements differently.

Monogamous relationships can include a wide variety of relationship dynamics, including BDSM, power exchange, dominant/submissive relationships, and other forms of kink.

Common characteristics include:

  • Romantic and sexual exclusivity, as mutually agreed.
  • Shared commitments and relationship expectations.
  • Emotional and practical support between partners.
  • Agreed-upon boundaries around outside relationships.
  • Potential long-term goals such as cohabitation, marriage, or family.

Potential opportunities: Some people appreciate the focus, stability, simplicity, and sense of exclusivity that monogamy provides.

Potential challenges: Partners may have different expectations around intimacy, sexual frequency, emotional needs, or personal independence. Monogamy does not automatically eliminate jealousy, conflict, or relationship difficulties.

Monogamish

Monogamish describes a relationship that is primarily monogamous but allows some agreed-upon flexibility around sexual or intimate experiences with other people.

The term was popularized by Dan Savage and is often used to describe couples who maintain a committed relationship while occasionally exploring experiences outside it.

Examples might include:

  • Occasional threesomes or group sexual experiences.
  • Limited sexual encounters with other people.
  • Exploring together at parties or lifestyle events.
  • Occasional outside experiences while traveling.
  • Specific exceptions to an otherwise exclusive relationship agreement.

Monogamish relationships generally maintain a primary romantic partnership and do not necessarily involve developing additional romantic relationships.

Potential opportunities: Couples may enjoy exploring new experiences while maintaining the familiarity and commitment of their existing relationship.

Potential challenges: Partners may disagree about what constitutes an acceptable exception. Expectations can become complicated if one person develops feelings for someone outside the relationship.

Open Relationships

An open relationship is a relationship in which partners agree that one or both may pursue sexual relationships or experiences with other people.

Open relationships can take many forms. Some couples permit sexual experiences but not romantic relationships, while others allow emotional or romantic connections as well.

Common arrangements include:

  • Sexual experiences with other people without romantic involvement.
  • Casual dating or sexual relationships outside the primary partnership.
  • Separate social or sexual experiences.
  • Joint experiences with other individuals or couples.
  • Relationships that allow emotional intimacy but have agreed limitations.

Potential opportunities: Open relationships can provide sexual freedom, opportunities for exploration, and flexibility around individual needs.

Potential challenges: Partners must navigate time, communication, jealousy, sexual health, expectations, and changing emotional connections.

An open relationship does not necessarily mean polyamory. The distinction often comes down to whether additional romantic relationships are included in the agreement.

Swinging

Swinging is a form of consensual non-monogamy that generally involves individuals or established couples engaging in sexual activities with other people, often as a shared recreational or social experience.

Swinging commonly takes place in social environments where people meet others with similar interests. These may include private parties, lifestyle clubs, resorts, or organized events.

Common forms include:

  • Partner swapping: Couples exchange partners for sexual experiences.
  • Soft swap: An arrangement in which couples agree to certain sexual activities while excluding others. Definitions vary.
  • Full swap: An arrangement permitting a broader range of sexual activities with other participants.
  • Group play: Multiple people participate in shared sexual experiences.
  • Same-room play: Partners engage with others while remaining together in the same environment.
  • Separate-room play: Partners agree to have separate experiences, sometimes in different rooms or spaces.

The specific meanings of soft swap, full swap, and other lifestyle terms can vary between communities and individuals. Partners should never assume that everyone uses the same definitions.

Potential opportunities: Swinging can provide shared exploration, social connection, sexual variety, and opportunities to meet people with similar interests.

Potential challenges: Differences in attraction, comfort levels, boundaries, performance expectations, and emotional responses can create tension between partners.

Swinging generally emphasizes shared sexual experiences rather than developing multiple romantic relationships. However, lasting friendships and romantic feelings can develop, and some people move between swinging and polyamory.

Polyamory

Polyamory is a form of consensual non-monogamy in which people have, or are open to having, multiple romantic and/or intimate relationships with the knowledge and consent of everyone involved.

Unlike arrangements that primarily focus on outside sexual experiences, polyamory generally makes room for multiple loving relationships.

Polyamorous relationships can involve two people who each have additional partners, larger interconnected relationship networks, or groups in which several people are romantically and/or sexually involved with one another.

There is no single polyamorous relationship model. Some people prioritize a primary partnership, while others avoid hierarchy and allow each relationship to develop according to its own needs and agreements.

Polyamory can involve sexual relationships, romantic relationships, or combinations of both. A person does not have to be sexually active with every romantic partner.

Potential opportunities:

  • Developing multiple meaningful romantic connections.
  • Building broader emotional and social support networks.
  • Experiencing different forms of intimacy with different people.
  • Learning more about communication, boundaries, and personal needs.
  • Building chosen families and interconnected communities.
  • Allowing relationships to develop without requiring one partner to fulfill every need.

Potential challenges:

  • Managing time and energy across multiple relationships.
  • Jealousy, insecurity, comparison, and fear of abandonment.
  • Negotiating boundaries and agreements between multiple people.
  • Balancing existing commitments with new relationships.
  • Managing differences in expectations and relationship priorities.
  • Handling changes in relationships and the impact on other partners.
  • Dealing with social stigma or lack of understanding from others.

Polyamory is not simply about having more partners. It requires attention to the needs, autonomy, and well-being of everyone involved.

The following sections explore polyamory in considerably more detail because it has its own terminology, structures, opportunities, and challenges.

Relationship Anarchy

Relationship Anarchy (RA) is a relationship philosophy that challenges traditional assumptions about how relationships should be organized and prioritized.

Rather than automatically ranking romantic relationships above friendships, or assuming that relationships must follow a particular progression, relationship anarchy emphasizes individual autonomy and mutually negotiated connections.

People practicing relationship anarchy may reject conventional expectations such as:

  • Romantic relationships automatically taking priority over friendships.
  • Marriage being the ultimate goal of a relationship.
  • Living together being necessary for commitment.
  • Sexual exclusivity being assumed without discussion.
  • Relationships needing to follow a predetermined progression.

Relationship anarchy does not necessarily mean having multiple sexual or romantic partners. Someone may practice relationship anarchy while having one romantic partner, several partners, or no romantic partners.

Potential opportunities: Greater freedom to create relationships that reflect the actual needs and desires of the people involved.

Potential challenges: People may have different expectations around commitment, responsibilities, priorities, and the meaning of a relationship. Rejecting conventional labels does not eliminate the need for agreements or accountability.

Understanding Polyamory in More Detail

Polyamory is a broad relationship category rather than a specific arrangement. People practice it in many different ways, and the structure of one person's relationships may look very different from another person's.

Some people maintain several independent relationships. Others build interconnected relationship networks in which partners know, socialize with, and sometimes develop friendships with one another.

Understanding the terminology helps people communicate what they are looking for and avoid making assumptions about how someone else's relationships operate.

Common Polyamorous Relationship Configurations

The following configurations describe how people may be connected. They are not rankings of relationship importance or measures of commitment.

V Relationship

A V relationship involves three people, with one person maintaining a relationship with two other people who are not romantically or sexually involved with one another.

The person connected to both partners is often called the hinge partner.

For example, Alex may have a romantic relationship with both Jordan and Taylor, while Jordan and Taylor are not romantically involved with each other.

Triad or Throuple

A triad, sometimes called a throuple, is a relationship involving three people who are romantically and/or sexually connected.

In a fully connected triad, all three people have relationships with each other.

A triad does not necessarily mean that everyone has identical feelings, commitments, or sexual relationships. Each individual connection can be different.

Quad

A quad involves four people connected through romantic and/or sexual relationships.

A quad can take many forms. It might consist of two established couples who become involved with one another, or a more interconnected group where several people develop individual relationships.

Not everyone in a quad necessarily has a romantic or sexual relationship with every other person.

Polycule

A polycule is a network of people connected through polyamorous relationships.

The word combines the ideas of polyamory and a molecule. It describes the larger network rather than requiring every person to be romantically or sexually involved with everyone else.

A polycule might contain several couples, a triad, individual partners, and people who are connected indirectly through other relationships.

Hierarchical Polyamory

Hierarchical polyamory is a structure in which certain relationships are given greater priority or carry additional commitments compared with others.

Common terminology includes:

  • Primary partner: A partner who has a designated central or higher-priority role in someone's relationship structure.
  • Secondary partner: A partner whose relationship may have fewer shared commitments or less priority in certain areas.
  • Tertiary partner: A term sometimes used for a relationship with comparatively limited commitments or involvement.

Hierarchy can be explicit or implicit.

An explicit hierarchy may include negotiated agreements about living arrangements, finances, scheduling, or relationship decisions.

An implicit hierarchy may emerge because of marriage, children, shared finances, cohabitation, or the length of an existing relationship.

Hierarchical relationships are not automatically unhealthy. However, it is important to recognize how decisions affecting one partner may impact other partners.

Non-Hierarchical Polyamory

Non-hierarchical polyamory generally avoids assigning partners predetermined rankings based on labels such as primary or secondary.

Each relationship is considered individually, with commitments and expectations negotiated between the people involved.

Non-hierarchical does not mean every relationship receives identical time, attention, or resources. People may still have different responsibilities, histories, and levels of involvement.

For example, someone may live with one partner and have children with another while still valuing all their relationships without formally ranking them.

Solo Polyamory

Solo polyamory describes a relationship approach in which someone maintains their independence and autonomy while having multiple romantic relationships or remaining open to them.

Solo polyamorous people may not want a traditional primary partnership, shared household, or merged finances.

This does not mean they avoid commitment or meaningful relationships. It means they may define commitment differently from conventional relationship models.

Polyfidelity

Polyfidelity describes a closed relationship involving three or more people who agree to maintain romantic and/or sexual exclusivity within the defined group.

For example, three people may be committed to one another and agree not to pursue additional romantic or sexual relationships outside their group.

Polyfidelity combines aspects of group relationships with agreed exclusivity.

Kitchen Table Polyamory

Kitchen Table Polyamory (KTP) describes an approach in which partners and metamours are comfortable spending time together and may develop friendships or close social connections.

The term comes from the idea that everyone could comfortably sit around the same kitchen table.

This does not mean that everyone must date each other or become equally close. It simply describes a preference for an interconnected and friendly relationship network.

Garden Party Polyamory

Garden Party Polyamory is an arrangement in which metamours are comfortable being together at occasional social events but do not necessarily maintain close friendships or frequent contact.

For example, different partners may comfortably attend birthdays, celebrations, or gatherings together.

Parallel Polyamory

Parallel polyamory describes a structure in which a person's different relationships operate relatively independently.

Metamours may know about one another but have limited interaction or prefer to maintain separate social lives.

Parallel polyamory does not necessarily involve secrecy. It can simply reflect a preference for personal boundaries and independent relationships.

These approaches exist on a spectrum. People may move between parallel, garden party, and kitchen table arrangements as relationships develop.

Polyamory Terminology and Glossary

Polyamorous communities use a variety of terms to describe relationships, emotional experiences, and relationship agreements. Learning this vocabulary can make conversations significantly easier.

People and Relationships

  • Metamour: A partner's other partner, with whom you are not necessarily romantically or sexually involved.
  • Hinge: The person who maintains relationships with two partners who are not partnered with each other. This person is the connecting point in a V relationship.
  • Nesting partner: A partner with whom someone shares a home or primary living arrangement.
  • Anchor partner: A partner who provides an important source of stability, commitment, or emotional connection. This does not necessarily imply hierarchy.
  • Comet partner: A partner with whom someone has an ongoing connection despite infrequent contact, often because of distance, travel, or lifestyle.
  • Polycule: A network of people connected through polyamorous relationships.
  • Chosen family: A family-like support network built through meaningful personal connections rather than biological or legal relationships.
  • Unicorn: A term commonly used for a person, often a bisexual woman, sought by an established couple to join them romantically or sexually. The term can be controversial because it may imply that the individual is expected to satisfy the couple's predetermined expectations.
  • Unicorn hunting: The practice of an established couple seeking a third person, often with restrictive expectations about how that person must interact with both partners. It can create unequal power dynamics and should be approached thoughtfully.

Emotional Experiences and Concepts

  • Compersion: Experiencing happiness or joy because a partner is happy in another relationship. Sometimes described as the opposite of jealousy.
  • Jealousy: An emotional response that may involve insecurity, fear, perceived competition, or concern about losing an important relationship. Jealousy is not unique to non-monogamous relationships.
  • New Relationship Energy (NRE): The excitement, enthusiasm, and heightened attention often associated with a new romantic or sexual connection. NRE can influence decision-making and affect existing relationships.
  • Old Relationship Energy (ORE): The familiarity, comfort, stability, and accumulated history associated with established relationships.
  • Polysaturation: The point at which someone feels they have reached their capacity for additional relationships because of time, energy, emotional resources, or other responsibilities.
  • Relationship escalator: The conventional expectation that relationships should progress through a predictable sequence, such as dating, exclusivity, cohabitation, marriage, and children.
  • Relationship autonomy: The ability of individuals to make decisions about their own relationships while respecting their partners' autonomy.
  • De-escalation: Changing a relationship's expectations or commitments without necessarily ending the relationship. This might involve living separately or changing future plans.

Agreements and Communication

  • Ethical Non-Monogamy (ENM): An umbrella term for consensual relationship arrangements that allow romantic, sexual, or intimate connections outside an exclusive partnership.
  • Consensual Non-Monogamy (CNM): Another commonly used term for relationship arrangements involving mutually agreed non-exclusivity.
  • Boundary: A personal limit describing what someone is comfortable with and what they will or will not participate in.
  • Agreement: A mutually negotiated understanding about how people will conduct themselves within a relationship.
  • Veto power: An agreement that allows one partner to demand that another partner end an outside relationship. This can create significant power imbalances, particularly when the affected partner has no meaningful voice in the decision.
  • Don't Ask, Don't Tell (DADT): An arrangement in which partners agree not to discuss certain details of outside relationships. This requires careful negotiation and should not be confused with deception or lack of consent.
  • Parallel communication: A relationship approach in which information and interaction between separate relationships are deliberately limited while relevant agreements and safety information are still respected.
  • Kitchen table communication: An approach that encourages direct, open, and sometimes group-based communication among members of a relationship network.
  • Fluid bonding: A term sometimes used to describe agreements about sexual activities involving bodily fluids and barrier use. It should be clearly defined because different people use it differently.

These terms are descriptive tools, not mandatory labels. Individuals and communities sometimes use the same words differently. Always ask someone what a particular term means to them rather than assuming.

The Opportunities and Challenges of Polyamory

Polyamory can be rewarding, but it also requires considerable communication and relationship management. It is important to understand both the positive possibilities and the practical difficulties.

Opportunities

  • Multiple meaningful connections: People can develop different kinds of emotional, romantic, and sexual relationships.
  • Broader support systems: A larger network of partners and metamours may provide different forms of companionship and support.
  • Personal growth: Navigating multiple relationships can encourage self-reflection, communication, and emotional awareness.
  • Freedom from traditional expectations: People can define commitment and relationship milestones in ways that fit their lives.
  • Sexual and romantic compatibility: Different relationships can fulfill different interests and forms of intimacy.
  • Community: Some people find meaningful friendship, support, and chosen family within polyamorous communities.

Challenges

  • Time management: Multiple relationships require time, attention, and scheduling.
  • Jealousy and insecurity: People may experience fear of replacement, comparison, or unequal attention.
  • Unequal expectations: Partners may have different ideas about commitment, exclusivity, and relationship progression.
  • Relationship changes: A breakup or major change in one relationship can affect other members of a polycule.
  • Communication complexity: More people and more connections can mean more opportunities for misunderstandings.
  • Social stigma: People may encounter judgment, discrimination, or misunderstanding from friends, family, employers, or society.
  • Financial and household considerations: Living arrangements, parenting, finances, and long-term planning may require additional discussion.

Jealousy Is Not a Failure

One of the common misconceptions about polyamory is that successful polyamorous people do not experience jealousy.

This is not true. Jealousy is a normal human emotion that can occur in monogamous and non-monogamous relationships alike.

The goal is not necessarily to eliminate jealousy. Instead, it is important to understand what is contributing to the emotion and communicate about it constructively.

Jealousy may reflect:

  • Fear of abandonment or replacement.
  • Concerns about unequal time or attention.
  • Uncertainty about relationship expectations.
  • Previous relationship trauma.
  • Insecurity about one's own desirability or importance.
  • Unmet needs or broken agreements.

Sometimes the issue is not jealousy itself but a legitimate concern about how someone is being treated. Not every difficult emotion should be dismissed as something an individual simply needs to overcome.

New Relationship Energy

New Relationship Energy (NRE) deserves particular attention in polyamorous relationships.

When someone begins a new relationship, the excitement and attention associated with that connection can temporarily influence priorities and decision-making.

This can create challenges when an established partner feels neglected or when someone makes commitments during the excitement of a new relationship that are difficult to sustain later.

Helpful practices include:

  • Maintaining existing commitments and scheduled time.
  • Avoiding major decisions based entirely on initial excitement.
  • Communicating about changing needs and expectations.
  • Being realistic about available time and energy.
  • Remembering that established relationships still need attention.

Romantic, Sexual, Emotional, and Platonic Relationships

One of the most important distinctions in understanding relationships is that romantic attraction, sexual attraction, emotional attraction, and platonic connection are not necessarily the same thing.

Society often assumes that romantic relationships automatically involve sexual attraction, that sexual relationships should eventually become romantic, and that friendships are less important than romantic partnerships.

These assumptions do not reflect everyone's experiences. People can experience different types of attraction independently, and relationships can be meaningful without following conventional expectations.

Romantic Relationships

Romantic attraction involves a desire for romantic connection with another person.

What people consider romantic can vary considerably. For some, it involves dating, affection, kissing, romantic gestures, or building a life together. For others, romantic intimacy may look quite different.

Romantic relationships can include:

  • Dating and courtship.
  • Romantic affection and expressions of love.
  • Emotional intimacy and companionship.
  • Shared plans and commitments.
  • Long-term partnership or marriage.

Romantic relationships do not necessarily require sex. Some couples are romantic but not sexually active, while others have romantic relationships in which sexual activity is not a priority.

Sexual Relationships

Sexual attraction involves experiencing sexual interest or desire toward another person.

Sexual relationships can exist with or without romantic involvement.

Examples include:

  • Casual sexual relationships.
  • Friends with benefits.
  • Sexual relationships within open relationships.
  • Swinging and lifestyle connections.
  • Romantic relationships that also include sex.
  • Sexual relationships involving BDSM or power exchange.

Sexual intimacy does not automatically imply romantic commitment, exclusivity, or a desire for a traditional relationship.

Platonic Relationships

Platonic relationships are connections that are not based on romantic or sexual involvement.

Platonic relationships can be extremely meaningful and may involve deep emotional intimacy, trust, commitment, affection, and long-term support.

Examples include:

  • Close friendships.
  • Best friends who consider each other family.
  • Chosen family relationships.
  • Long-term companionship.
  • Deep emotional support relationships.
  • Some forms of mentorship and community connection.

A platonic relationship should not be interpreted as less important or less committed than a romantic relationship.

Emotional Attraction and Intimacy

Emotional attraction involves feeling drawn to someone because of their personality, emotional connection, understanding, or shared experiences.

Emotional intimacy involves being able to share personal thoughts, feelings, vulnerabilities, and experiences with another person.

Emotional attraction and intimacy can exist in romantic, sexual, and platonic relationships.

Someone might have a very close emotional connection with a friend, a romantic connection with a partner, and a primarily sexual connection with another person.

Other Forms of Attraction

Attraction can also be experienced in ways that do not fit neatly into the categories above.

  • Aesthetic attraction: Finding someone visually appealing without necessarily wanting romantic or sexual involvement.
  • Sensual attraction: Wanting physical closeness or contact, such as cuddling, holding hands, or affectionate touch, without necessarily wanting sexual activity.
  • Intellectual attraction: Being drawn to someone's ideas, knowledge, curiosity, or way of thinking.
  • Alterous attraction: A type of attraction that may not fit comfortably into conventional romantic or platonic categories.
  • Spiritual attraction: Feeling drawn to someone through shared beliefs, values, or spiritual experiences.

These categories are descriptive concepts rather than strict scientific classifications. Individuals may experience attraction in different ways or choose not to use these labels at all.

Asexuality, Aromanticism, and the Split Attraction Model

Understanding the differences between romantic and sexual attraction is particularly important when discussing asexuality and aromanticism.

Asexuality

Asexuality is a sexual orientation characterized by experiencing little or no sexual attraction to other people.

Asexuality exists on a spectrum. Some people experience no sexual attraction, while others experience it rarely or under particular circumstances.

Asexuality does not necessarily mean:

  • Someone does not want romantic relationships.
  • Someone never engages in sexual activity.
  • Someone does not experience physical pleasure.
  • Someone cannot enjoy intimacy or affection.
  • Someone cannot participate in kink or BDSM.

Asexual people may have romantic relationships, participate in BDSM, enjoy sensual activities, or have different preferences around sex.

Aromanticism

Aromanticism describes experiencing little or no romantic attraction to other people.

Like asexuality, aromanticism exists on a spectrum. Some people experience no romantic attraction, while others experience it rarely or under particular circumstances.

Aromantic people may still desire:

  • Sexual relationships.
  • Close friendships and companionship.
  • Long-term committed partnerships.
  • Physical affection and intimacy.
  • Family relationships and chosen family.

The Split Attraction Model

The Split Attraction Model is a framework often used in asexual and aromantic communities to distinguish romantic attraction from sexual attraction.

It recognizes that these forms of attraction do not necessarily align.

For example:

  • Someone may experience romantic attraction but little or no sexual attraction.
  • Someone may experience sexual attraction but little or no romantic attraction.
  • Someone may experience both romantic and sexual attraction toward the same person.
  • Someone may experience neither romantic nor sexual attraction.

This framework helps explain why people may want different things from their relationships and why assumptions about sex and romance can be misleading.

It is a useful conceptual tool, not a requirement that everyone categorize their experiences in this way.

Queerplatonic Relationships

A queerplatonic relationship (QPR) is a committed or significant relationship that does not fit neatly into conventional romantic or platonic categories.

These relationships can involve deep emotional intimacy, commitment, shared living arrangements, long-term plans, and other forms of partnership.

Queerplatonic relationships are particularly recognized within aromantic and asexual communities, although people with other identities may also use the term.

A queerplatonic partnership may be more significant or committed than what society traditionally expects from a friendship, without being understood by the people involved as romantic.

Relationships, Kink, and BDSM

Kink and BDSM can exist within virtually any relationship structure. Being kinky does not automatically mean someone is non-monogamous, and being polyamorous does not automatically mean someone participates in BDSM.

These are separate aspects of someone's interests, identity, and relationships.

Dominant and Submissive Relationships

Some people incorporate BDSM power exchange into romantic relationships, while others maintain separate D/s connections.

For example:

  • A monogamous couple may have an ongoing dominant/submissive dynamic.
  • Someone may have a romantic partner and a separate D/s partner.
  • Someone may have multiple romantic relationships with different power-exchange dynamics.
  • A person may participate in occasional scenes without seeking an ongoing relationship.
  • Someone may have a platonic leather family or chosen family with shared protocols and rituals.

Relationship Agreements and BDSM Agreements

Relationship agreements and BDSM agreements serve different purposes, although they may overlap.

Relationship agreements may address:

  • Romantic and sexual exclusivity.
  • Dating and additional relationships.
  • Time commitments and communication.
  • Safer-sex practices and health considerations.
  • Living arrangements and financial commitments.

BDSM agreements may address:

  • Roles and power exchange.
  • Protocols and expectations.
  • Activities and limits.
  • Safewords and consent.
  • Privacy and confidentiality.
  • Aftercare and ongoing communication.

A person being someone's Dominant does not automatically give them authority over that person's other relationships. Similarly, being someone's submissive does not eliminate their ability to negotiate their relationship boundaries.

Power exchange should never be used to bypass consent or force someone into a relationship arrangement.

Polyamory and Power Exchange

Polyamory and BDSM can intersect in particularly interesting ways.

A person might have a romantic partner who is also their Dominant, another partner with whom they share a different D/s dynamic, and additional relationships that are entirely outside BDSM.

These connections may have different expectations, commitments, and boundaries.

It is important to negotiate which aspects of a power-exchange dynamic apply to which relationships. A protocol established with one Dominant should not automatically be assumed to apply to another partner.

Building Healthy Relationships

Regardless of relationship structure, healthy relationships generally require honesty, communication, mutual respect, and a willingness to address problems.

Communication

Discuss expectations rather than assuming everyone understands a relationship in the same way.

Boundaries

Understand the difference between personal boundaries and attempts to control another person's behavior. Agreements should be mutually negotiated and revisited when circumstances change.

Honesty

Be transparent about commitments, expectations, and circumstances that affect other people. Consent depends on people having relevant information.

Time and Attention

Recognize that relationships require time and effort. Be realistic about what you can offer rather than making commitments that cannot be maintained.

Conflict Resolution

Disagreements are normal. Focus on understanding what is happening rather than assigning blame. Listen to the people affected and work toward solutions that respect everyone's needs.

Ongoing Consent

Relationship agreements are not permanent permission. People can change their minds, renegotiate boundaries, and decide that a relationship no longer works for them.

Respect for Different Relationship Styles

Monogamy, polyamory, swinging, and other relationship styles are different ways of organizing relationships. None is inherently superior.

A healthy relationship is not defined by the number of partners involved or whether it follows traditional expectations. It is defined by the quality of the connections and how people treat one another.

Recommended Books and Resources

The following resources offer additional perspectives on relationship structures, polyamory, attachment, communication, and relationship diversity.

Books About Polyamory and Non-Monogamy

1. Polysecure: Attachment, Trauma and Consensual Nonmonogamy
Jessica Fern

One of the most useful resources for understanding attachment and emotional security in non-monogamous relationships.

Fern explores how attachment patterns, past experiences, and trauma can influence relationships involving multiple partners. The book introduces frameworks for developing greater security and connection.

Particularly useful for understanding jealousy, attachment, emotional needs, and relationship security.

Publisher information: Polysecure

2. Polywise: A Deeper Dive into Navigating Open Relationships
Jessica Fern and David Cooley

A companion to the concepts introduced in Polysecure, with additional attention to navigating the practical and emotional complexities of open relationships.

Particularly useful for people who are already exploring non-monogamy and want to develop stronger relationship skills.

Thornapple Press – Polywise and related books

3. More Than Two, Second Edition: Cultivating Nonmonogamous Relationships with Kindness and Integrity
Eve Rickert and Andrea Zanin

A substantial guide to non-monogamous relationships that examines communication, boundaries, autonomy, power, fairness, and the practical realities of maintaining multiple relationships.

The second edition substantially reworks the original book and offers a more contemporary approach to non-monogamy.

Particularly useful for understanding relationship ethics, hierarchy, autonomy, and the responsibilities people have toward their partners.

Publisher information: More Than Two, Second Edition

4. The Ethical Slut, Third Edition
Janet W. Hardy and Dossie Easton

A longstanding resource covering consensual non-monogamy, communication, sexual exploration, jealousy, and relationship ethics.

The book explores a broad range of nontraditional relationship arrangements and provides guidance on communicating desires and navigating interpersonal dynamics.

Publisher information: The Ethical Slut

5. Opening Up: A Guide to Creating and Sustaining Open Relationships
Tristan Taormino

A practical exploration of different non-monogamous relationship arrangements, including open relationships, swinging, polyamory, and other alternatives to traditional monogamy.

Particularly useful for people who are beginning to explore alternatives to exclusivity.

6. Love's Not Color Blind: Race and Representation in Polyamorous and Other Alternative Communities
Kevin A. Patterson

Explores race, representation, diversity, and inclusion within polyamorous and alternative relationship communities.

An important resource for understanding how social identity and broader cultural experiences influence relationship communities.

Final Thoughts

Relationships are personal, and people have different needs, expectations, and definitions of intimacy. What works for one person may not work for another.

Monogamy, swinging, open relationships, polyamory, relationship anarchy, and other structures all offer different possibilities and challenges.

It is equally important to recognize that romantic, sexual, emotional, and platonic relationships can exist independently or overlap in different ways.

You do not need to fit into a particular relationship category or follow a conventional path. You can define your relationships around the connections you want to build and the commitments you are comfortable making.

Ultimately, healthy relationships are built on honesty, mutual respect, informed consent, communication, and a willingness to understand the people with whom we share our lives.
